\section{Summary of findings}

This thesis compared two ways of optimizing rule-based trading strategies. The standard approach
optimizes directly on the historical IS period. The alternative approach optimizes on multiple
synthetic paths generated from that same IS period. Both approaches are then tested on the same real OOS
period.

The results answer two different questions. First, whether the synthetic paths are a reasonable
approximation of the real IS market. Second, whether optimizing on those paths reduces the IS/OOS
performance gap.

\section{Synthetic markets as realistic path approximators}

On the first question, the results are positive. When the same benchmark parameters were evaluated on
the synthetic IS paths for MA Cross, the mean synthetic performance remained close to the real IS
performance. I did not repeat this same-parameter validation for Std MRev, because
Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic-threshold-gbm} already shows that the threshold-constrained paths remain close
to the original market path. The generator therefore seems to produce paths that remain connected to
the original market, rather than unrelated random walks with the same starting price.

\section{Does synthetic-market optimization reduce overfitting?}

On the second question, the result is weaker. In these experiments, synthetic-path optimization does not
show a clear reduction in overfitting. Across the two strategies:

\begin{itemize}
    \item For MA Cross, the synthetic workflow produced a larger net profit gap than the standard
    workflow (\(-57.94\) versus \(-38.49\)). The Sharpe gap was slightly smaller, but the OOS Sharpe was
    still worse.
    \item For Std MRev, the standard workflow had a positive Sharpe gap, while the synthetic workflow
    had a negative Sharpe gap of \(-0.14\).
\end{itemize}

The expected pattern would be a consistently smaller IS/OOS gap under the synthetic workflow. That
pattern does not appear. With two strategies and one asset, this is not enough to reject the idea, but
it is enough to say that the evidence in this thesis does not support it.

The two findings should be kept separate. The synthetic paths behave reasonably well as market
approximations. However, this does not automatically mean that optimizing on them improves OOS
performance.

\section{Limitations}

Several limitations matter for the interpretation of these results.

All experiments were run on a single asset, AAPL, over a single historical window and a single 70/30
IS/OOS split. Strategy performance and IS/OOS gaps can change across assets and market regimes.

All experiments used a fixed position size of one lot rather than a fractional or equity-scaled sizing
rule. Because AAPL trades at higher prices in the OOS period, dollar metrics such as net profit and
average trade are affected by the price level. This is why Sharpe and max DD percentage were given more
weight in the discussion.

Parameter selection at every stage, in both the historical and synthetic workflows, relied on visual
inspection of grid plots for robust regions rather than automatic maximization. This was intentional,
but it remains partly discretionary.

The synthetic generator itself has one free parameter, the threshold multiplier \(k\) (the number of
rolling standard deviations allowed around the original price), which was fixed at \(k = 10\)
throughout and not optimized. The volatility window was likewise fixed at 30 bars. Both choices affect
the generated paths, so the results are conditional on these settings.

In theory, synthetic optimization could allow a larger part of the original historical series to be
kept for real-data testing, because parameters are selected on generated paths rather than directly on
the real path. This thesis does not use that design. A 70/30 split is kept so that the synthetic
workflow can be compared directly with the standard IS/OOS benchmark. Also, any data used to calibrate
or anchor the synthetic generator should not be treated as fully OOS.

The optimizer is a plain grid search and is not parallelized. This constrained how fine and how wide
the parameter grids could be. In the synthetic workflow, each parameter combination is tested on every
synthetic path, so using 50 paths means running 50 backtests for one grid point.

\section{Future work}

A natural extension is to repeat the comparison across more assets, more historical windows, and more
strategies. This would show whether the pattern observed here is general or specific to this dataset.
Using fixed-fractional sizing would also remove the price-level issue in dollar metrics.

On the synthetic-generation side, it would be useful to treat the threshold multiplier and the
volatility window as parameters to test, instead of fixed constants. The generator should also be
compared with other synthetic-data methods, such as block bootstrapping of historical returns.

Finally, the optimizer should be parallelized before this method is scaled further. At the moment, a
synthetic optimization with 50 paths runs 50 backtests for each parameter combination. Given that its
OOS deterioration was not more predictable than the benchmark workflow in these experiments, the extra
computation is not clearly justified yet. Parallelization would make wider grids and more synthetic
paths practical, reducing noise without making the runtime excessive.
